Eigenvalues and Applications

Mathematics for Economists · 14 problems · short solutions after each question

Eigenvalues: \(Av=\lambda v\) and \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\). For transition matrices, \(z_{t+1}=Pz_t\), columns are origins, and a stationary distribution satisfies \(Pr=r\) (so \(\lambda=1\)).

A · Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

For each matrix, solve \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\), then find a nonzero vector satisfying \((A-\lambda I)v=0\).

1. Diagonal matrix

Find the eigenvalues and an eigenvector for each: \[A=\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}.\]
Solution
\(\det(A-\lambda I)=(3-\lambda)(2-\lambda)=0.\) Thus \(\boxed{\lambda=3,\ v=(1,0)^T}\) and \(\boxed{\lambda=2,\ v=(0,1)^T}\).

2. Symmetric matrix

Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of \[A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}.\]
Solution
\((2-\lambda)^2-1=(\lambda-3)(\lambda-1)=0.\) Eigenpairs: \(\boxed{3:(1,1)^T}\) and \(\boxed{1:(1,-1)^T}\).

3. Nonsymmetric matrix

Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of \[A=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{pmatrix}.\]
Solution
\((4-\lambda)(3-\lambda)-2=(\lambda-5)(\lambda-2)=0.\) For \(\lambda=5\), \(y=x\), so \(v=(1,1)^T\). For \(\lambda=2\), \(y=-2x\), so \(v=(1,-2)^T\).

4. Triangular matrix

Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of \[A=\begin{pmatrix}2&3\\0&5\end{pmatrix}.\]
Solution
\((2-\lambda)(5-\lambda)=0.\) The eigenpairs are \(\boxed{2:(1,0)^T}\) and \(\boxed{5:(1,1)^T}\).

5. Negative eigenvalue

Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of \[A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.\] What does a negative eigenvalue do?
Solution
\(\lambda^2-1=0\). Eigenpairs: \(\boxed{1:(1,1)^T}\) and \(\boxed{-1:(1,-1)^T}\). The second vector reverses sign: \(Av=-v\).

6. Zero eigenvalue

Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of \[A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}.\] Is \(A\) invertible?
Solution
\((1-\lambda)(4-\lambda)-4=\lambda(\lambda-5)=0\). Eigenpairs: \(\boxed{0:(-2,1)^T}\) and \(\boxed{5:(1,2)^T}\). The matrix is singular because \(\det A=0\).

7. Symmetric 3×3 matrix

Find every eigenvalue, counting multiplicities, and three independent eigenvectors of \[A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&1\\1&2&1\\1&1&2\end{pmatrix}.\]
Solution
\(\det(A-\lambda I)=(1-\lambda)^2(4-\lambda)=0\), so \(\boxed{\lambda=4,1,1}\). For \(4\): \((1,1,1)^T\). For \(1\): two independent choices are \((1,-1,0)^T\) and \((1,0,-1)^T\).

8. Triangular 3×3 matrix

Find the eigenvalues and one eigenvector for each of \[A=\begin{pmatrix}3&2&1\\0&5&4\\0&0&7\end{pmatrix}.\]
Solution
\(\det(A-\lambda I)=(3-\lambda)(5-\lambda)(7-\lambda)\), giving \(\boxed{3,5,7}\). Eigenvectors: \(3:(1,0,0)^T\), \(5:(1,1,0)^T\), \(7:(5,8,4)^T\).

B · Migration between two cities

Write \(z_{t+1}=Pz_t\), where \(z_t=(x_t,y_t)^T\) contains the populations of Belfast and Bangor. Each column of \(P\) is an origin and each row is a destination.

9. Symmetric migration

Initially Belfast has 600 residents and Bangor 400. Every year, 20% of each city's residents move to the other city. Construct \(P\); find next year's populations, eigenvalues and eigenvectors, stationary populations (total 1,000), and populations in year 2.
Solution
\[P=\begin{pmatrix}.8&.2\\.2&.8\end{pmatrix},\qquad P\binom{600}{400}=\binom{560}{440}.\] \(\det(P-\lambda I)=(\lambda-1)(\lambda-.6)=0\). Eigenpairs: \(1:(1,1)^T\), \(.6:(1,-1)^T\). Steady populations: \(\boxed{(500,500)^T}\). Year 2: \((536,464)^T\). The gap \(200\to120\to72\) shrinks by a factor of \(0.6\).

10. Asymmetric migration

Initially Belfast has 600 residents and Bangor 400. Every year, 10% leave Belfast for Bangor, while 20% leave Bangor for Belfast. Construct \(P\); find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors, the stationary population (total 1,000), and populations after two years.
Solution
\[P=\begin{pmatrix}.9&.2\\.1&.8\end{pmatrix},\quad P\binom{600}{400}=\binom{620}{380},\quad P\binom{620}{380}=\binom{634}{366}.\] \(\det(P-\lambda I)=(\lambda-1)(\lambda-.7)\). Eigenpairs: \(1:(2,1)^T\), \(.7:(1,-1)^T\). Stationary population \(\boxed{(2000/3,1000/3)^T}\). Deviations shrink by 30% each year.

11. Overshooting and a negative eigenvalue

Each year, 80% of Belfast residents move to Bangor and 60% of Bangor residents move to Belfast. Initially populations are \((700,0)^T\). Find \(P\), its eigenpairs, the stationary population and the first two years. Interpret a negative eigenvalue.
Solution
\[P=\begin{pmatrix}.2&.6\\.8&.4\end{pmatrix},\quad\det(P-\lambda I)=(\lambda-1)(\lambda+.4).\] Eigenpairs: \(1:(3,4)^T\) and \(-.4:(1,-1)^T\). Stationary population \((300,400)^T\); trajectories \((700,0)\to(140,560)\to(364,336)\). The negative eigenvalue reverses the deviation's sign each year; its absolute value \(.4\) makes it shrink.

C · PageRank

Google faced a ranking problem: thousands of webpages could contain the same search terms. Which should appear first? A link from a well-connected, important webpage ought to count more than a link from an obscure one. PageRank models users following links and finds the steady visitor shares. The resulting ranking solves \(Pr=r\): an eigenvector corresponding to \(\lambda=1\).

12. Three webpages

Page A links equally to B and C; B links only to A; C links only to B. Construct the column-stochastic \(3\times3\) matrix \(P\). Starting from equal shares, compute the shares after one click. Explain why PageRank uses \(\lambda=1\), then solve \((P-I)r=0\) and \(a+b+c=1\).
Solution
\[P=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\1/2&0&1\\1/2&0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad P\begin{pmatrix}1/3\\1/3\\1/3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1/3\\1/2\\1/6\end{pmatrix}.\] At stationarity \(Pr=r\), so \(\lambda=1\). Equations \(a=b,\ c=a/2,\ a+b+c=1\) give \(\boxed{r=(2/5,2/5,1/5)^T}\). A and B tie for first.

13. Which incoming links are more valuable?

Now A links equally to B and C; B links only to A; C links equally to A and B. Write \(P\), find the one-click distribution from equal shares, then find and interpret the eigenvector for \(\lambda=1\), normalised to sum to 1.
Solution
\[P=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&1/2\\1/2&0&1/2\\1/2&0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad P\begin{pmatrix}1/3\\1/3\\1/3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1/2\\1/3\\1/6\end{pmatrix}.\] From \((P-I)r=0\): \(c=a/2\), \(b=3a/4\). Normalising gives \(\boxed{r=(4/9,1/3,2/9)^T}\). Ranking: A, then B, then C; a link's contribution depends on its source's visitor share.

14. Random jumps

A links only to B, B links only to A, and C links only to A. First solve \((P-I)r=0\). Then suppose visitors follow a link with probability \(1/2\) and jump uniformly to A, B or C with probability \(1/2\): \(T=\frac12P+\frac12U\), where all entries of \(U\) equal \(1/3\). Find \(T\) and solve \((T-I)r=0\), with shares summing to 1.
Solution
\[P=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&1\\1&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad r_P=(1/2,1/2,0)^T.\] \[T=\begin{pmatrix}1/6&2/3&2/3\\2/3&1/6&1/6\\1/6&1/6&1/6\end{pmatrix}.\] \((T-I)r=0\) gives \(c=1/6,\ b=a/2+1/6\), so \(\boxed{r_T=(4/9,7/18,1/6)^T}\). Random jumps give C a positive rank and help prevent link traps and cycles.
